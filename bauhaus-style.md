The vocabulary is deliberately tiny. Three primaries, red, yellow, and blue, plus black and
white, printed on warm off-white paper. Three shapes, the circle, the triangle, and the
square, used at poster scale and allowed to overlap. Wassily Kandinsky, who taught at the
school, ran a questionnaire pairing each shape with a colour, triangle with yellow, square
with red, circle with blue, and that pairing still turns up in work quoting the style
ninety years later. Type is geometric sans with no ornament and no serif, set flush left
against an asymmetric grid, frequently in all lowercase because Herbert Bayer argued that
two alphabets for one sound was waste. Rules and bars organise the page, and a single
diagonal supplies the movement that the right angles refuse.

The school itself ran from 1919 to 1933, founded by Walter Gropius in Weimar, moved to
Dessau in 1925 and briefly to Berlin, and closed under Nazi pressure. Its project was to put
art back into production: workshops taught by artists and craftsmen together, with the aim
that a chair, a teapot, a building, and a poster could all be designed by the same
principles. Its own slogan was art and technology, a new unity. The phrase most often
attached to it now, form follows function, is the architect Louis Sullivan's from 1896,
adopted rather than coined in Dessau, and the school's teachers argued about it constantly.
When the staff scattered in 1933 they took the method to Chicago, to Ulm, and eventually to
almost every design curriculum that followed.

Its descendant on this site is [Swiss style](/swiss-style), and the two are easy to blur.
Swiss design inherited the grid, the sans serif, the asymmetry, and the refusal of ornament,
then dropped the primaries and the shape play in favour of photography, a neutral grotesque,
and the mathematics of the grid itself. So a page of red circles and yellow triangles is
Bauhaus quoting; a page of Helvetica flush left on a twelve column grid with one photograph
is Swiss. De Stijl is the other close relative, sharing the primaries but keeping to
rectangles and horizontals, which is exactly why a diagonal reads as a Bauhaus move.

As an interface register it suits identity work, marketing pages, and anything that wants to
look like a considered object: the shapes make excellent empty states, section markers, and
category marks. Two cautions. Saturated primaries are already spoken for in an interface,
where red is an error and blue is a link, so a poster palette applied to a form will say
things you did not mean, and shape coding needs a text label beside it rather than colour
alone. And geometric sans faces are drawn for display: at body sizes their closed apertures
and single storey letterforms slow reading down, so borrow the grid and the discipline but
set the paragraphs in something meant for paragraphs.
