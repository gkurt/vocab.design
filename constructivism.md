This is a specific movement with a specific address: Soviet Russia, roughly 1919 to 1934. It
began as an argument inside the post-revolutionary avant-garde that easel painting was a
bourgeois indulgence, and that the artist should become a constructor making useful things
for a new society: posters, book covers, packaging, textiles, kiosks, theatre sets. The
people who made it are named, and their work is worth looking at before borrowing from it:
Alexander Rodchenko, Varvara Stepanova, El Lissitzky, Gustav Klutsis, and the Stenberg
brothers, whose film posters are where most of the visual moves people copy today were
worked out.

The diagonal is the part that matters and the part that is usually taken without its
meaning. Setting a bar, a block of type, or a whole composition on an angle was a claim
about dynamism and machinery, and a rejection of the calm centred symmetry that older
official art used to say that the order of things was permanent. El Lissitzky's 1919 poster
of a red wedge driving into a white circle is the clearest single statement of it: the
diagonal is the argument, and the geometry is doing the talking. Photomontage carried a
related claim, since a cut photograph reads as documentary evidence in a way a painted
figure does not. The palette was also a constraint before it was a taste: cheap presses,
cheap paper, and often only two inks, which is why so much of it is red and black on a warm
off-white.

On a screen the register survives as a small set of moves. Drive the composition with one
strong diagonal rather than several, since two competing angles read as noise. Set the
heavy sans capitals as a structural element, along the bar rather than beside it. Keep the
palette to two inks plus paper. And treat the rules as construction, at real weight, not as
hairlines. The practical cost is that a rotated block of text is harder to read and much
harder to make responsive, so the honest arrangement is angled display type over
horizontally set body copy.

It is easy to confuse with its neighbours because they share a decade and some people.
[De-stijl](/de-stijl) is the opposite temperament: strictly orthogonal, primary, and calm,
with no diagonal permitted anywhere. [Bauhaus-style](/bauhaus-style) is the school that
absorbed a lot of this thinking when Lissitzky and Laszlo Moholy-Nagy carried it west, but
it is a teaching programme rather than a political one. [Swiss-style](/swiss-style) is the
orderly descendant: the asymmetry, the sans type, and the photography all survive into it,
while the shouting does not. And [brutalist-web-design](/brutalist-web-design) borrows the
raw look without any of the construction, which is exactly the difference. If you use the
register, use it knowing that the angle was a political argument in a specific country at a
specific moment, and that reproducing period slogans is a different act from borrowing a
layout.
