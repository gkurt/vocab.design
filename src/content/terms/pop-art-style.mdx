The recipe is a comic page enlarged until its manufacture shows. Colour is flat and
primary, red and yellow and blue with black and white, and there are no gradients and no
tints because a cheap press had none. Every shape carries the same heavy black outline, so
nothing recedes. Shading is done with dots, blown up until each one is a circle rather than
a texture. Words are part of the picture: speech balloons with tails, rectangular caption
boxes, and onomatopoeia set in bold condensed capitals, often outlined twice and tilted.
And motifs repeat, four or nine or sixteen times in a grid with the colourway swapped, which
turns an image into a pattern and a portrait into a product.

The movement came before the look. It was named in Britain in the mid 1950s, where Richard
Hamilton and the Independent Group were already collaging advertising and packaging, and it
became a New York phenomenon in the 1960s. Two artists supply almost everything the style
now borrows. Roy Lichtenstein enlarged single comic panels, hand painting the printer's dots
at a scale where the reader sees the process rather than the picture, and Andy Warhol
screenprinted commercial subjects in flat colour, repeated in grids with the inks changed.
So a pop art page today is usually Lichtenstein's panel with Warhol's repetition, plus a
starburst borrowed from a supermarket flyer.

Two neighbours here need separating. [Halftone](/halftone) is the printing technique itself,
the conversion of continuous tone into dots of varying size, and it is a tool that any style
can use, including photographic ones; pop art is a whole visual language that wears enlarged
halftone dots as decoration and would still be recognisable in solid colour.
[Neubrutalism](/neubrutalism) shares the thick black outline and the hard offset shadow, but
it is an interface style with no dots, no balloons, and no comic lettering, and it applies
those devices to buttons and cards rather than to pictures.

As an interface register it is loud on purpose and works where loudness is the message:
promotions, event pages, games, anything for an audience that reads the reference. What it
does badly is carry text. A dot field behind body copy destroys contrast and produces moire
when the page is zoomed or the screen is dense, so keep the dots to blocks of flat colour
and leave the reading surface plain. Condensed [capitals](/all-caps) are slow to read in
runs longer than a few words, which is fine for POW and fatal for a paragraph. And an
outline on everything is an outline on nothing: if every element gets the same three pixel
black border, the page loses the [visual hierarchy](/visual-hierarchy) that tells a reader
which of them is the button.
