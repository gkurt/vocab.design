Maximalism is not an absence of editing, which is the thing that separates it from a
page that simply got away from its author. The moves are deliberate and they repeat:
several saturated hues used at full strength rather than one accent with tints, a pattern
behind the content instead of a flat fill, type mixed across faces and sizes so a display
weight sits next to something italic, elements rotated off the grid, stickers and badges
overlapping the boxes they belong to, and shadows offset in colours that are not shadows.
Every layer is doing something, and the layering itself is the message.

The current wave arrived as a reaction. After roughly a decade in which every product
site converged on white space, one sans, and a single blue, the look had become
indistinguishable, and abundance became the cheapest way to be recognisable. Vernacular
followed: "dopamine design" for the saturated, high-energy version popular with consumer
apps, "cluttercore" for the deliberately overstuffed domestic variant that came out of
interiors, and "Gen-Z chaos" for the layered, sticker-covered register borrowed from
music and streetwear. Poster design, zine layout, and early personal-website culture are
all being quoted, often knowingly.

The craft is in what stays fixed while everything else moves. Legible maximalism keeps
body copy on a plain patch with real contrast, keeps one clear primary action, and keeps
the reading order intact even when the decoration ignores the grid. The failure mode is
that nothing is quiet, so nothing can be emphasised: when every element is loud, the
call to action has to win on size or position rather than on standing out, and a reader
scanning for it has no low-contrast background to scan against. Motion, pattern, and
saturation together are also a genuine accessibility load, so the style owes its
audience contrast checks, a reduced-motion path, and restraint about how much of it
animates at once.

It is a style with a shelf life per page rather than per product. Maximalism carries a
landing page, a launch announcement, or an editorial feature well, and it wears badly on
anything a person has to use every day, which is why the products that adopt it usually
keep the abundance at the front door and a quieter interface behind it.
