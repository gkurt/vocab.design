Two devices carry the whole style. The first is vibrating colour: complementary hues set at
nearly the same lightness, orange against blue, red against green, so the eye cannot settle
on the boundary between them and the edge appears to shimmer and crawl. The second is
lettering treated as a fluid. Words are drawn rather than set, stretched and squeezed until
they fill whatever shape has been left for them, an ellipse, a wave, a keyhole, with the
counters closed up and the letters interlocking so the block reads as an object before it
reads as a word. Around them go concentric contour bands, kaleidoscopic symmetry, and
liquid distortion, and the leftover space is filled rather than left empty, because empty
space would let the eye rest.

It came out of San Francisco between roughly 1966 and 1968, mostly through dance posters for
the Fillmore and the Avalon Ballroom. Wes Wilson drew the melting lettering the style is
known for; Victor Moscoso, who had studied colour under Josef Albers at Yale, broke the
teaching deliberately by pairing complementaries of equal value to make his posters buzz;
Rick Griffin, Stanley Mouse, and Alton Kelley filled out the group. The debt to Art Nouveau
is direct: a large exhibition of the earlier style toured American museums in 1965 and the
poster artists took its whiplash curves and its integrated lettering straight off the wall,
which is why a Fillmore poster and an [Art Nouveau](/art-nouveau) panel share a silhouette.
Difficulty was part of the design. If a passer by could not read the poster, it was not
addressed to them, and taking the time to decode it was the price of admission.

On this site the lineage continues into [acid graphics](/acid-graphics), and the pair is
worth holding apart. Psychedelia is analogue and hand drawn, its warping done with a brush
and a photostat camera, its palette built from inks that fight each other on paper. Acid
graphics is the digital rave descendant: the same refusal of legibility rendered in chrome
and mesh distortion, over near black rather than over saturated colour, and ironic where the
originals were sincere. The [vaporwave](/vaporwave) and [Y2K](/y2k-aesthetic) revivals both quote the
1960s at second hand, usually through the acid branch rather than directly.

Interfaces can borrow the composition but not the colour arithmetic. Vibrating pairs are the
one part that does not survive translation: text set that way is a
[contrast ratio](/contrast-ratio) failure by definition, since equal lightness is exactly
what the calculation measures, and the shimmer that reads as energy on a printed poster
reads as discomfort on a backlit screen and can be genuinely painful for readers prone to
visual stress or migraine. Use it for a hero image, an event page, or cover art where the
type is large, decorative, and duplicated as real text elsewhere. Keep the running copy in
something readable, and let the poster be a poster.
