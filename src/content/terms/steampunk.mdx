The materials are the style. Brass and copper plate held down with visible rivets, dark
stained wood, oiled leather, smoked glass, and a little verdigris where the metal has been
handled. Controls are mechanical and oversized: pressure gauges with painted faces and a
red needle, valve wheels, brass levers in slotted tracks, toggle switches with guards, and
exposed cogs turning behind a window. Type is Victorian, which means fat slab serifs,
engraved copperplate, and ornamental borders, usually printed on aged cream rather than
white. The unifying rule is that nothing is concealed: every mechanism is on the outside
where it can be seen working, which is the opposite of the sealed slab a modern device is.

It is the nineteenth century branch of [retro-futurism](/retro-futurism), which names it
as one of its children. The move is the same one every branch of that family makes: build
tomorrow out of an older decade's materials and let the anachronism do the work. Here the
older decade is the age of steam, so the future runs on boilers, brass, and clockwork, and
the worldview that comes attached is Victorian too, all industry, ornament, exploration,
and a faith that a machine you can see the insides of is a machine you can understand and
repair. Against [cassette futurism](/cassette-futurism) the difference is a hundred years
of manufacturing: beige injection moulded plastic, paddle switches, and small amber tubes
on one side, hand fitted brass and glass on the other, both of them imagining a future out
of the parts their own period could actually make.

In interfaces it belongs to games, escape rooms, tabletop and fiction sites, event
branding, and the occasional maker or coffee brand. The failure mode is worth naming
plainly. Every one of these controls is a signifier borrowed from a machine that gave
force feedback, and a brass lever drawn on a screen gives none, so a reader is asked to
learn a widget that behaves nothing like it looks. Dark brass with cream engraving is
usually low contrast, ornamental borders eat space that small screens do not have, and a
gauge is a poor readout when a number would do. The version that survives contact with a
product keeps the material in the frame and the illustration, uses one gauge where a gauge
is genuinely the right instrument, and lets the working controls be ordinary controls.
