Fake urgency is a deadline with nothing behind it. The banner says the offer ends in
four minutes and fifty-nine seconds, the digits move, and the reader feels the clock
even if they never read the words. Then they refresh the page and it is four fifty-nine
again. Nothing was expiring. The timer was a pressure device rendered as a fact, and the
decision it hurried was one the reader had all week to make. The same trick runs at
larger scales: a sale that has been ending on Sunday every Sunday for two years, a
"today only" price that is the only price the product has ever had, a booking screen
that starts a fifteen-minute hold on a seat nobody else is looking at.

The honest instrument it imitates is the [countdown timer](/countdown-timer), and the
difference is not in the pixels. A real countdown reports something the system will
enforce whether or not anyone is watching: a shipping cutoff that moves the delivery
date, a session that signs you out, a held seat that goes back into inventory, a
one-time code that stops working. Reload the page and an honest clock picks up where it
was, because it is counting toward an event that exists outside the browser tab. The
test is that simple, and it is the reason a team can usually tell which one they have
shipped without arguing about intent: either something changes at zero, or the clock is
the only thing that does.

It sits under the [dark pattern](/dark-pattern) umbrella, catalogued at
[deceptive.design](https://www.deceptive.design) alongside its twin,
[fake scarcity](/fake-scarcity). The two pressure the same decision from different
directions: fake urgency invents a limit on time, fake scarcity invents a limit on
quantity ("only 2 left", counted by nobody). Teams often ship them together, a countdown
above a stock badge, because the combination reads as a market rather than as a
marketing decision.

Regulators now treat manufactured deadlines as a deceptive practice rather than as
enthusiasm: the US Federal Trade Commission has acted on false urgency claims, and the
UK Competition and Markets Authority names urgency and pressure selling in its guidance
on online choice architecture. The design rule that keeps a team clear is to make the
clock earn its place. Name the deadline, name what changes when it passes, let it
survive a reload, and if nothing at all happens at zero, write the offer as a sentence
and let the reader decide at their own speed.
