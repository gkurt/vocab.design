Universal navigation is the thin strip above the navigation, the one carrying the parent
organisation's name and a short row of links to its other sites. Every large university,
government department and enterprise group has one, most readers have used one this week, and
almost nobody building them calls it anything. That is the whole reason it earns an entry: an
unnamed piece of chrome is a piece of chrome nobody owns, nobody measures, and nobody can argue
about, and the argument is worth having.

It exists for a reader who never saw the parent site. Most arrivals at a subsite come from a
search result and land three levels deep, with no idea that the department belongs to a
university, that the university has a library, or that the thing they actually want lives on a
sibling site. A universal bar answers that in one line: this is whose page you are on, and here is
the rest of it. It also gives the parent organisation a way to be present without redesigning
every subsite, which is usually the real reason it gets mandated.

The cost is honest and worth stating plainly. The bar spends the most valuable strip on the page,
the one above the fold and above the actual navigation, on links that analytics almost always show
nobody clicks. It gives the page two stacked navigations, which makes the visual hierarchy harder:
a reader has to work out which of two bars belongs to the thing they came for. And it lengthens
the path to the content for anyone tabbing or using a screen reader, which is a
[skip link](/skip-link) problem before it is a design problem, since the skip target has to clear
both bars rather than one. Keeping the bar genuinely slim and genuinely quiet, smaller type and
lower contrast than the nav below it, is what makes the trade acceptable.

Keep the three layers apart when reviewing one. [Global navigation](/global-navigation) is the
subsite's own top level: the sections a reader came here for.
[Local navigation](/local-navigation) is the level below that, within a section. Universal
navigation is the level above both, and it belongs to somebody else, which is why it is the one
that most often gets designed by committee and shipped without a purpose. The test is simple: name
the reader it serves and the one link they need. If the answer is the organisation's own homepage
and nothing else, a single line in the footer may do the same job for none of the cost.
