Every composition answers the question "where do I look first", whether or not anyone
decided the answer. A focal point is the deliberate answer: one element given enough
weight that the eye reaches it before it reaches anything else, with everything else
arranged to send it there. On a landing page that is usually the promise and the button
under it, in a photograph it is the face, on a dashboard it is the number that tells you
whether to worry.

Weight comes from a small set of levers, and they compose. Size is the crudest and the
most reliable: the largest thing wins unless something else is working harder. Contrast is
the cheapest, since one saturated element in an otherwise quiet field is found before
anything has been read, which is why a single accent goes further than three. Isolation is
the most underrated: space around an element separates it from its neighbours, and a
button crowded by links reads as one of the links. Position, depth, and direction (a face
looking at something) do the rest. One lever pulled properly is usually enough. All of
them pulled at once is a shout.

Two failures sit either side of the term. The first is having no focal point at all:
everything the same size, the same weight, the same distance apart, so the page becomes a
field the eye samples and abandons. The second is having several, which is the same
failure in louder clothing, because three elements competing for first place leave the
reader to arbitrate. [Visual hierarchy](/visual-hierarchy) is the whole ranking of a
layout; the focal point is only its first rank, and a layout with no rank one has no
hierarchy worth the name.

The vocabulary arrives from painting and photography, where the same idea is often called
the center of interest, and it survives the move to interfaces because the perceptual
claim survives. It travels into motion too: the [Ken Burns effect](/ken-burns-effect) is a
slow move that keeps a still image's focal point where the frame wants it, and a pan that
loses it reads as drift. The cheapest test is to squint at a layout, or look at it as a
thumbnail, which strips away everything except relative weight and shows whether one thing
still steps forward.
