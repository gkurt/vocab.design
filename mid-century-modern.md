The period runs roughly from 1945 to 1969, and the look comes out of what postwar
manufacturing had just learned to do: mould plywood, cast fibreglass, print laminate, and
sell all three to a young household that had never owned furniture before. That optimism
shows up in the drawing. Colour is warm but knocked back, mustard and teal and burnt orange
rather than red and blue, because the reference is dyed fabric and printed laminate rather
than ink. Shapes taper instead of ending square: a leg narrows toward the floor, a table top
becomes a kidney, a lamp becomes a cone. Motifs come from the science pages of the same
decade, so a pattern is a starburst, an atom, a boomerang, or an amoeba, drawn as thin lines
rather than filled illustration. Type is geometric sans, often set in caps with generous
tracking, and it is friendly rather than authoritative.

Three drawing moves carry most of the register on a screen. Put the layout on a ground with
some tooth in it, a paper or linen texture rather than flat white, because a period print was
never on a perfectly smooth surface. Let one or two shapes taper or curve while everything
else stays rectangular, since the style is geometry with an organic exception rather than
organic throughout. And keep the palette muted and close in value, which is also where the
trouble is: mustard on cream is a handsome pairing and a contrast failure, so the text ink
has to stay dark even when the fills do not. The amoeba and kidney forms belong to the same
family as [blob-shape](/blob-shape), which is what the move became once it lost its period.

It is easy to confuse with the modernist registers on either side of it.
[Bauhaus-style](/bauhaus-style) is earlier, stricter, and colder, a teaching programme whose
primaries and circles are arguments about form rather than furnishings.
[De-stijl](/de-stijl) is stricter still, orthogonal and primary with no warmth and no curve
anywhere. [Swiss-style](/swiss-style) is the later, systematic descendant, a grid and a
neutral sans with the decoration removed on purpose. And
[memphis-design](/memphis-design) is the direct rebellion against exactly this taste: the
same shapes turned loud, clashing, and deliberately ungracious, precisely because mid-century
modern had by 1980 become the safe good taste of an older generation.

Googie is the roadside branch of the same period, mostly Southern Californian architecture:
upswept roofs, cantilevers, boomerang signage, neon, and starbursts scaled to be read from a
moving car. It is named after a Los Angeles coffee shop, and it pushes the atomic-age motifs
much harder than the domestic version does, which is why it reads as space age where a
Herman Miller catalogue reads as calm. Worth knowing before you reach for the register at
all: this is now one of the most commercially worn looks available, so a screen built from
starbursts and mustard alone lands as a diner pastiche. The version that still works borrows
the restraint, one tapered shape and a warm ground, rather than the souvenirs.
